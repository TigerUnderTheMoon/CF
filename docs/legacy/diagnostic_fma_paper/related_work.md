# Related Work

## Reflection, Deliberation, and Self-Correction

Reflection-oriented agent methods motivate the study of explicit reflective operations. Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) and Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) evaluate whether critique, feedback, and revision improve trajectory-level task performance. Broader reasoning methods such as Chain-of-Thought, Zero-shot Chain-of-Thought, Self-Consistency, Least-to-Most prompting, ReAct, Tree of Thoughts, Graph of Thoughts, and STaR show that intermediate reasoning, deliberation, tool use, and bootstrapped rationales can improve final-answer behavior (Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023a; Yao et al., 2023b; Besta et al., 2024; Zelikman et al., 2022).

FMA asks a different question from these generative or prompting methods. It does not try to elicit better trajectories. It studies reflective spans after they exist in a recorded trace and asks whether locally useful spans behave as topology-sensitive structural support. This makes FMA complementary to reflection and deliberation methods: those methods improve or sample reasoning processes, while FMA diagnoses how reflective components are organized under controlled functional and graph perturbations.

## Process Supervision and Step-Level Signals

Process Reward Models and verifier-style systems provide the closest supervision comparison. Process supervision for mathematical reasoning, advantage-style process feedback, automated step verification, Training Verifiers, and Math-Shepherd all share the goal of assigning value below the final-answer level (Lightman et al., 2023; Uesato et al., 2022; Cobbe et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024). FMA shares the step-level motivation, but it does not assume that every locally useful step is a valid supervision target.

The distinction matters because process supervision can overfit easy-to-score intermediate signals. A local reflective span may correlate with utility while remaining redundant in the surrounding trace structure. FMA therefore treats local utility as a candidate signal that must be checked against operational structural necessity, redundancy, compensation, and bottleneck evidence before it is used as a supervision or filtering weight.

## Attribution, Interpretability, and Faithfulness

Counterfactual and attribution-based interpretability methods provide the methodological background for controlled perturbation. LIME, Integrated Gradients, SHAP, saliency sanity checks, and attention-faithfulness debates show both the usefulness and fragility of attribution-style explanations (Ribeiro et al., 2016; Sundararajan et al., 2017; Lundberg and Lee, 2017; Adebayo et al., 2018; Jain and Wallace, 2019; Wiegreffe and Pinter, 2019). ERASER and faithfulness-oriented NLP evaluation further emphasize that explanation quality depends on the unit of analysis and the evaluation protocol (DeYoung et al., 2020; Jacovi and Goldberg, 2020).

FMA differs from token, attention, or feature attribution because its unit is an explicit reflective span. The framework asks whether observable self-evaluation, planning, verification, or error-diagnosis spans have intervention-sensitive local utility and whether that utility survives graph-level stress tests. This shift from token-level explanation to reflective-span diagnostics makes the method more directly relevant to process supervision, while keeping the interpretation bounded to operational proxies.

## Counterfactual Language and Non-Identification

The paper uses intervention language in a deliberately limited sense. Causal-inference references clarify what is not being claimed: FMA does not estimate Rubin-style causal effects, average treatment effects, or hidden mechanism recovery (Rubin, 1974; Pearl, 2009; Hernan and Robins, 2020). Counterfactual explanation work is relevant because it studies how outputs change under controlled alternatives, but FMA applies that logic to trace and graph objects rather than to a full decision model (Wachter et al., 2017).

This non-identification boundary is important for terminology. The reported quantities are functional and structural proxies: `attribution_score` measures local functional contribution under a fixed scoring contrast, and `structural_necessity` measures topology-sensitive dependence under deterministic graph operations. The paper therefore uses terms such as intervention-sensitive utility, operational structural necessity, and topology-sensitive dependence rather than true causal effect.

## Graph Diagnostics and Structural Necessity

The graph layer draws on the general idea that node removal, propagation, and rerouting reveal different forms of structural dependence. Network science has long distinguished direct node vulnerability, cascade behavior, and robustness under attacks or failures (Albert et al., 2000; Motter and Lai, 2002; Newman, 2010). Graph explanation work also treats substructures and connectivity patterns as interpretable evidence, though FMA applies graph diagnostics to reflective traces rather than graph neural network decisions (Ying et al., 2019).

PRUNE, CASCADE, and BYPASS are therefore not arbitrary failure modes. PRUNE tests direct node indispensability, CASCADE tests downstream propagation dependence, and BYPASS tests substitutability or rerouting through neighboring structure. Together they form a minimal graph-stress-test basis for operational structural necessity: direct dependence, propagated dependence, and alternative-route dependence. The trio is not an exhaustive theory of reasoning, but it is sufficient to test whether local utility can be collapsed into structural necessity.

## Knowledge Systems, Metacognitive Diagnostics, and Structured Attribution

FMA's diagnostic analysis of reflective operations in language-agent traces connects to a longer tradition in knowledge systems research. Three intersecting lines of work provide relevant precedent and theoretical grounding.

**Cognitive Architectures and Metacognition.** Classic cognitive architectures embedded metacognitive monitoring as a separable architectural concern long before language-model agents. SOAR's impasse-driven learning mechanism triggers deliberate subgoal reasoning when routine processing fails, explicitly separating automatic execution from reflective problem-solving (Laird, 2012) [XX]. ACT-R's conflict-resolution and utility-learning modules evaluate production-rule selection with explicit cost-benefit signals, enabling the architecture to monitor and adjust its own processing preferences (Anderson et al., 2004) [XX]. These architectures treat metacognition as a built-in control loop. FMA shares the functional motivation—understanding how reflective operations contribute to reasoning—but operates on a fundamentally different unit of analysis: explicit reflective spans in language-model traces rather than architectural sub-systems. This shift makes FMA's diagnostics applicable to black-box language agents, where internal mechanisms are inaccessible, but it also means that FMA cannot claim that a span corresponds to an architectural loop. It can only ask whether a recorded reflective segment, under controlled perturbation, behaves as a locally useful or structurally necessary component of the observable trace [XX].

**Process Verification in Knowledge-Based Systems.** The question of step-level necessity has a longer history in knowledge-based systems. MYCIN's explanation facility traced rule-firing chains backward from conclusions to evidence, making intermediate inference steps transparent and interrogable to clinicians (Buchanan and Shortliffe, 1984) [XX]. These explanation traces encoded the dependency structure of the knowledge base and allowed users to ask why a particular intermediate conclusion was reached. Modern knowledge graph reasoning extends this tradition: path-ranking algorithms, logical rule mining over knowledge graphs, and explainable link prediction treat reasoning paths as inspectable objects that carry varying evidential weight (Lao et al., 2011; Yang et al., 2017) [XX]. FMA's graph diagnostics—PRUNE, CASCADE, and BYPASS—echo this concern but transpose it from rule chains to reflective spans in language-agent traces. Where MYCIN asked whether a fired rule was necessary for a clinical conclusion, FMA asks whether a recorded reflective span is structurally necessary within its surrounding trace graph. Both traditions share the intuition that not every intermediate reasoning step is equally indispensable, and that explicit dependency structure reveals which steps carry genuine inferential weight [XX].

**Structure Attribution in Neuro-Symbolic AI.** Neuro-symbolic reasoning systems provide a third line of relevant precedent. Neural theorem provers, differentiable logic programming, and logic neural networks embed symbolic reasoning operators within neural architectures and increasingly evaluate whether intermediate proof steps, unification operations, or logical inference rules contribute to successful theorem completion (Rocktaschel and Riedel, 2017; Evans and Grefenstette, 2018; Manhaeve et al., 2018) [XX]. These systems naturally separate local step utility from global proof-structure necessity: a locally plausible intermediate lemma may be structurally irrelevant if the proof can complete via an alternative path, while an apparently minor unification may be structurally critical. FMA echoes this local/structural separation in the language-agent domain. Its distinction between `attribution_score` (local functional contribution) and `structural_necessity` (topology-sensitive dependence) mirrors the neuro-symbolic insight that step utility and proof-structure necessity are distinct quantities that must be calibrated and reported separately rather than collapsed into a single signal [XX].

These three traditions together reinforce the intellectual frame for FMA's core diagnostic question: when an intermediate reasoning operation is recorded in an observable trace, when is it locally useful and when is it structurally necessary? FMA answers with operational diagnostics over stored trace graphs rather than with architectural or logical commitments, which makes the framework applicable to black-box language agents while limiting its structural claims to the observable trace level.

## Reproducibility, Benchmarks, and Data Governance

Reproducibility work in machine learning emphasizes that reported results depend on documented data, code, seeds, variance, and experimental reporting choices (Pineau et al., 2021; Dodge et al., 2019; Bouthillier et al., 2019). Benchmark design work also cautions against reading a single static score as a general capability claim. GLUE, SuperGLUE, Dynabench, HELM, and benchmark-governance critiques motivate careful scenario design, coverage analysis, and explicit limits on single-score interpretation (Wang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019; Kiela et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2022; Raji et al., 2021).

Dataset and model documentation work gives the paper its provenance discipline. Datasheets, data statements, model cards, dataset-development surveys, corpus documentation, dataset versioning, deduplication, and contamination diagnostics all show that evaluation claims depend on what data were selected, consumed, repeated, or excluded (Gebru et al., 2021; Bender and Friedman, 2018; Mitchell et al., 2019; Paullada et al., 2021; Dodge et al., 2021; Lhoest et al., 2021; Hugging Face, 2026; Lee et al., 2022; Golchin and Surdeanu, 2023; Yang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). FMA adopts that discipline at the trace level: results are reported relative to a stored task distribution, fixed perturbation protocol, and explicit structural diagnostic layer.
