# Introduction

Reflective reasoning is often treated as a source of improved language-model problem solving. A model checks an intermediate result, revises a plan, decomposes a task, or critiques a prior step; the final answer may improve, and the reflective step is then treated as evidence that reflection helped. This view is intuitive, but it leaves an important structural question open: Do reflective reasoning steps that appear locally useful also exhibit structural necessity?

This paper studies that question through intervention-based functional attribution for reflective cognition dynamics. The central distinction is between local utility and structural necessity. A reflective step may have a positive local attribution signal because deterministic counterfactual scoring associates it with an improved outcome. The same step may still have zero measured structural necessity if the surrounding reflective graph can preserve downstream utility when the step is removed, bypassed, or absorbed by neighboring structure. Conversely, a step with modest local attribution may occupy a topology-sensitive position. The goal is not to collapse these signals into one score, but to study their mismatch.

Much work on chain-of-thought and reflection-oriented reasoning focuses on local utility signals, trajectory-level improvement, or step-level quality. These signals are important, but they do not answer whether reflection is uniformly essential. A reasoning trace can contain many locally functional reflective steps without requiring most of them to be structurally necessary. If this occurs, reflection is not best described as a uniformly indispensable layer. It is better described as a heterogeneous set of local operations whose topology-sensitive dependence varies across the observable trace.

The project therefore separates three levels of claim. The empirical observations are deterministic outputs: attribution scores, necessity scores, alignment diagnostics, redundancy density, compensation ratios, distributedness, and resilience curves. The structural interpretation is that local utility is more widespread than structural necessity in the stored traces. Possible implications for process supervision, reflection pruning, or trace compression are future directions, not settled conclusions.

This separation matters for paper evaluation because a high local score can be over-read. If a reflective step receives a positive local utility estimate, one might infer that it should be retained, supervised, or treated as structurally necessary. The Phase 6 and Phase 7 results caution against that shortcut. Local utility answers a within-context functional question; structural necessity asks whether topology-sensitive dependence remains after deterministic removal or bypass. A paper that merges the two would overstate the evidence and obscure the main finding.

The contribution is therefore organizational as well as empirical. The repository already contains attribution analysis, intervention analysis, topology analysis, redundancy analysis, limitation analysis, and a deterministic evaluation pipeline. This manuscript fixes the terminology, claim hierarchy, figure inventory, reproducibility narrative, and limitation framing needed for a compact workshop paper. It does not introduce a new metric, structure-learning method, hidden-state model, learned model, or experiment phase.

The empirical core of the paper is Phase 5-7. Phase 5 estimates counterfactual functional attribution over synthetic reflective trajectories and produces step-level `attribution_score` and necessity-score records. Phase 6 converts reflective traces into graph-structured representations and measures `structural_necessity` under PRUNE, CASCADE, and BYPASS interventions. Phase 7 analyzes redundancy, weak compensation, rerouting, sparse bottlenecks, resilience, and distributedness over stored Phase 6 artifacts. Phase 1-4 provide the conceptual framework and deterministic infrastructure, but they are not presented as separate experimental contributions.

The resulting pattern is consistent across the repository outputs. Phase 6 reports weak Pearson alignment between local attribution and structural necessity: 0.0753 for PRUNE, 0.0523 for CASCADE, and 0.0917 for BYPASS. Structural necessity is zero-inflated: 67.79 percent of node-level structural necessity values are zero, and 49.54 percent of steps have positive local attribution with zero measured structural necessity. Phase 7 reports compensation and distributedness weaker than the initial hypothesis expected. Redundancy density is moderate at 0.3842, but the distributedness index is only 0.2976, mean compensation ratios are low, and sparse bottlenecks account for 191 of 2400 reflective nodes.

Stage 2 is used only as a held-out consistency check over stored artifacts. The full Stage 2 set reports a small aggregate Spearman signal, but the artifact-level claim labels for C1 rank generalization, C2 projection robustness, and C3 stratified generalization are all `stratum_dependent`. The confirmation gate does not pass across all four strata (`S_high`, `S_mid`, `S_low`, and `S_rand`), so these claims must not be described as globally confirmed.

The final research claim is therefore conservative: reflective reasoning exhibits widespread local utility, but only sparse structural necessity. Most reflective steps are neither necessary under the reported protocol nor strongly compensatory. Weak attribution-necessity alignment does not invalidate reflective utility attribution. Instead, it suggests that many reflective steps are locally functional, few are structurally necessary under the protocol, and compensatory redistribution is limited. Required baseline families remain unavailable in the held-out step-level comparison space, so the journal submission remains blocked rather than ready.

This framing is intentionally non-causal in the strong sense. The framework uses deterministic interventions and structural operations as operational proxies over observable reasoning traces. It does not recover hidden reasoning, semantic understanding, internal mechanisms, or protocol-independent effects. Its value is narrower and more reproducible: it provides an auditable organization of the evidence that local reflective utility and topology-sensitive necessity are distinct.
